Can this study support the headline?

Study: Researchers survey U.S. college students once. Students report their daily hours on social media and complete a depressive-symptoms questionnaire. Heavier users report more symptoms.

Three questions reveal three different gaps

Construct

Do “hours” capture meaningful social-media use?

Do symptom scores equal clinical depression?

Internal

Could depression increase social-media use?

Could loneliness or stress cause both?

External

Would this hold beyond U.S. college students, these platforms, and this period?

Better measurement, causal identification, and broader reach are different achievements.

Timing: 3–4 minutes. Use this slide to organize and sharpen the answers from the room.

Construct validity: Total time treats active conversation, passive scrolling, news, entertainment, support groups, and harassment as the same exposure. The content, mode, intensity, and social meaning of use may matter more than duration. A depressive-symptoms scale can be a valid measure of symptoms without establishing a clinical diagnosis. Improvements might include device logs, content or activity measures, validated multi-item symptom scales, clinical assessment, or convergent measures.

Internal validity: A one-time survey does not establish temporal order. Depression might increase withdrawal into social media; loneliness, stress, offline isolation, sleep disruption, family circumstances, or prior mental health could affect both use and symptoms. A longitudinal design, credible natural experiment, randomized intervention, baseline adjustment, or evidence that distinguishes competing pathways could strengthen causal inference. Simply adding controls does not automatically solve the problem.

External validity: The relevant scope includes people, platforms, patterns of use, outcomes, settings, and historical periods. College students may differ from adolescents or older adults; platform features and norms differ; and effects may depend on whether use is active, passive, supportive, or hostile. Ask students to name a specific boundary and why it could change the relationship—not merely say that the sample is “unrepresentative.”

Close by stressing that success on one ground does not guarantee success on the others. Excellent measurement cannot turn a cross-sectional association into a causal effect. A strong causal design may still study a narrow treatment or population. Replication across populations cannot repair a measure that does not represent the intended construct.

Abend’s problem

“Theory” is central to what we do—but scholars use the word to name different things.

Semantic confusion becomes an intellectual and institutional problem.

Timing: begin the 20-minute Abend segment. “Theoretical” often does both descriptive and favorable evaluative work, affecting publication, prestige, and what counts as a contribution.

Source: Abend (2008), abstract and pp. 173–177, 191–193.

Four different questions

Semantic
What does “theory” mean?

Ontological
What is theory?

Evaluative
What makes a theory good?

Teleological
What is theory for?

Abend directly answers the semantic question. His seven meanings provide a descriptive, lexicographic account of what competent sociologists mean by “theory” in different contexts. They are not seven species shown to share one established essence.

He does not provide one general answer to the other three questions:

  • Ontological: He does not tell us what theory essentially is. Asking for one essence before specifying the intended meaning risks the “Socratic error.” The objects called theory1, theory2, and theory5 may be fundamentally different kinds of things.
  • Evaluative: He does not give one standard of good theory. Appropriate standards depend on the contribution claimed: a theory1 proposition, theory2 explanation, and theory3 interpretation cannot simply be judged by the same criteria.
  • Teleological: He does not assign theory one purpose. The seven meanings perform different jobs, including generalizing, explaining, interpreting, reconstructing texts, orienting inquiry, making normative arguments, and examining philosophical problems.

Abend then introduces another question: What ought “theory” to mean in sociology? He calls this the semantic predicament. He does not resolve it by choosing one of the seven meanings. Instead, he recommends semantic therapy—authors and reviewers should specify the meaning they intend—and argues that any disciplinary settlement is partly political and should preserve ontological and epistemological pluralism.

Teaching shorthand: Abend answers what “theory” means in sociological usage; that answer complicates what theory is, what makes it good, and what it is for; he then asks what it ought to mean and who gets to decide.

Source: Abend (2008), pp. 174–177, 191–196.

What is a stylized fact?

A simple empirical regularity in need of explanation

It may be an:

association · incidence rate · trend

It says that a pattern exists—not why it exists.

Source: Hirschman (2016), pp. 604–608.

A stylized fact is a special kind of description

Observations
Individual exposure and knowledge scores

↓

Descriptive result
In this sample, heavier social-media news users averaged lower knowledge scores.

↓

Stylized fact
Social-media news use is negatively associated with political knowledge.

↓

Explanation
Fragmented presentation and incidental exposure reduce sustained processing and learning.

All stylized facts are descriptions—but not all descriptions are stylized facts.

The boundary between description and stylized fact is gradual rather than absolute. A stylized fact remains descriptive because it claims that an empirical regularity exists; it does not yet tell us why the regularity exists. But it is a particular kind of description: simplified, abstracted, portable, and presented as something that merits explanation.

Start with observations. Individual people have particular exposure measures and knowledge scores. These observations are the empirical materials. They are not themselves a stylized fact because no regularity has yet been extracted from them.

A descriptive result stays relatively close to a study or dataset. For example: “In this sample, respondents who reported heavier social-media news use answered an average of 4.2 knowledge questions correctly, compared with 5.1 among other respondents.” This reports what was observed in a particular sample, with a particular measure, at a particular time. It may be important, but it does not necessarily claim a broader or especially revealing pattern.

A stylized fact abstracts from those details. “Social-media news use is negatively associated with political knowledge” suppresses the particular sample means, platforms, questions, effect size, period, and exceptions. That simplification is the “stylizing.” The claim extracts a recognizable empirical tendency that can travel across papers, datasets, and public debates.

The move also does more than shorten the sentence. It constructs and stabilizes categories—“social-media news use” and “political knowledge”—and treats them as comparable across people and possibly across studies. It characterizes one relationship as the important pattern in the evidence. It therefore says, implicitly: This is a meaningful way to characterize the social world, and this pattern deserves explanation.

An explanation takes the next step. It identifies how or why the pattern depends on other things. For example: fragmented presentation and incidental exposure may reduce sustained cognitive processing, which reduces learning. That explanatory account could be correct or incorrect; the stylized fact itself remains agnostic about it.

Use these contrasts:

  • A description may report any observation; a stylized fact identifies a regularity.
  • A description may remain tied to one dataset or case; a stylized fact abstracts from particulars so the claim can travel.
  • A description need not claim broad importance; a stylized fact selects a pattern as worthy of scholarly attention.
  • A description need not create an explanatory puzzle; a stylized fact is explicitly or implicitly “in search of explanation.”
  • A stylized fact is usually simpler than the evidence supporting it and may omit qualifications, heterogeneity, and exceptions.

This does not mean that ordinary descriptions are free of concepts, interpretation, or value judgments. Hirschman’s point is not that stylized facts uniquely contain interpretation while all other descriptions transparently reproduce reality. Rather, stylized facts make the movement from observation to selected, generalized, explanation-seeking regularity especially visible.

Ask students to compare these statements:

  1. “Forty percent of the posts in this sample mentioned immigration.”
  2. “Immigration dominates political communication on social media.”
  3. “Immigration dominates because threat-oriented content receives greater algorithmic amplification.”

The first is a study-specific descriptive result. The second could function as a stylized fact if supported as a broader regularity and presented as an important characterization. The third proposes an explanation. Ask what empirical and conceptual work would be required to move responsibly from one statement to the next.

Teaching shorthand: A description says what was observed. A stylized fact extracts a simplified regularity and says, “This is the pattern that deserves explanation.” An explanation says why the pattern occurs.

Source: Hirschman (2016), especially pp. 604–609.

Stylized facts occupy an unstable middle

description ← stylized fact → theory

minimal interpretation ← stylized fact → maximal interpretation

fact ← stylized fact → value

Hirschman’s point is not merely that stylized facts contain “a little theory.” They occupy these middle positions because producing one requires moving beyond individual observations while stopping short of a full explanation.

Between description and theory: A stylized fact describes an empirical regularity—for example, “the share of income going to the top 1 percent increased.” But that statement is not a transparent transcription of raw data. Researchers must decide that income, tax units, and the top 1 percent are meaningful analytical categories; operationalize them; abstract from individual observations and measurement complications; and summarize the evidence as one recognizable pattern. This is theoretical work. Yet the stylized fact remains agnostic about why the pattern occurred. It is therefore more than a report of data but less than a causal theory or full explanation: a lightly theorized description.

Between minimal and maximal interpretation: A minimal interpretation stays close to what the evidence records: “The measured income share of the top 1 percent rose during this period.” A maximal interpretation might explain the increase through policy change, elite political power, labor-market restructuring, or another developed causal account. The stylized fact sits between them when it says, in effect, “The most revealing way to characterize recent inequality is growing concentration at the very top.” It interprets what the evidence is about and competes with other characterizations—such as racial or gender wage gaps, poverty, or the college wage premium—without yet explaining the selected pattern. This is why Hirschman places stylized facts near Abend’s theory3: they offer a way of making sense of an empirical phenomenon, though they are often presented as merely descriptive.

Between fact and value: A stylized fact makes a positive claim: this regularity exists in the world. It simultaneously makes a normative claim: this regularity is important enough to occupy scarce scholarly and political attention and therefore deserves explanation. “Normative” here does not necessarily mean that the researcher morally approves or condemns the pattern. It means that selecting one regularity as the important characterization necessarily directs attention away from other possible summaries. Epistemic values also enter: researchers judge one characterization to be simpler, more revealing, more important, or more useful for inquiry than its competitors.

Use the communication example: “Social-media news users have lower political knowledge” is empirical because it claims an association. It is lightly theorized because “social-media news use” and “political knowledge” must be constructed and operationalized, and the observations must be abstracted into a general pattern. It is interpretive because it presents political knowledge as the consequential way to understand social-media news use rather than, for example, participation, source diversity, trust, or political efficacy. It is normative because it asks the field to treat this relationship as worthy of further attention and explanation. It is not yet an explanation because it does not tell us why the association exists.

Teaching shorthand: A stylized fact says, “This is the pattern, this is the useful way to characterize it, and this is what deserves explanation”—but not yet, “This is why it happens.”

Source: Hirschman (2016), pp. 605, 608–609.

Why does explanation matter?

Explanation → understanding → often better prediction and control

McCain treats understanding via explanation as science’s primary epistemic aim.

McCain distinguishes two objects of understanding

Understanding a phenomenon

Grasp a correct explanation of how or why it occurs.

Understanding a scientific theory

Use it to construct or appreciate explanations or predictions in its domain.

McCain distinguishes understanding a phenomenon from understanding a scientific theory because they are different cognitive achievements, even though they often support one another.

Understanding a phenomenon means grasping a correct explanation of how or why that phenomenon occurs. The phenomenon may be a particular event, a recurring pattern, or a process. Understanding can involve grasping its causes, mechanisms, constitutive relations, governing principles, or relevant counterfactual dependence—how the phenomenon would have changed if something else had been different. Merely knowing that the phenomenon occurred, or knowing a regularity that predicts it, is not yet this kind of understanding.

McCain’s example is variation among Galápagos finches. A student might know that the finches differ in beak form. The student understands the phenomenon when they grasp how natural selection, environmental differences, and variation within populations produced that diversification. The explanation connects the fact to a larger structure of dependence.

Understanding a scientific theory means possessing the ability to use that theory to construct, recognize, or appreciate explanations and predictions within its domain. It is not merely being able to repeat the theory’s name or definition. Someone understands evolutionary theory to the degree that they can use it to explain phenomena such as finch variation or generate predictions about where transitional fossils might be found.

The distinction also explains why understanding comes in degrees. An informed layperson may appreciate an explanation after it is presented; an expert may be able to generate a new explanation, compare it with alternatives, identify its limits, and derive further predictions. Both may understand the theory, but not to the same depth.

How the two relate: Understanding a theory is often a resource for understanding phenomena. We exercise our understanding of a theory when we use it to build or appreciate an explanation. But the two achievements are not identical. A person can know a theory’s vocabulary without successfully explaining a particular phenomenon. Conversely, understanding one phenomenon does not imply the ability to use the theory across its entire domain.

Apply the distinction to the running communication example:

  • Understanding the phenomenon: Grasp why tolerance increased in this deliberative forum—for example, because exchanging reasons led participants to recognize opponents as legitimate citizens.
  • Understanding the relevant theory: Be able to use a broader account of deliberation, recognition, or intergroup contact to construct and evaluate explanations of this and other communication settings, identify when the account should apply, and derive further expectations.

Connect back to Abend. McCain uses “scientific theory” to mean a well-established domain of science containing strongly supported facts, methods, laws, or principles that can be used to explain and predict. This is a particular use of the word within McCain’s philosophical account. It does not settle Abend’s semantic predicament or show that all seven sociological meanings of “theory” are the same kind of object. Ask students: Which of Abend’s meanings seem closest to the work McCain expects a scientific theory to do, and what might be lost by forcing an exact one-to-one mapping?

Teaching shorthand: Understanding a phenomenon means being able to answer “Why this?” Understanding a theory means being able to use an organized body of knowledge to answer—and sometimes generate—many such questions.

Source: McCain (2022), chapter 1, pp. 7–9.

Prediction and explanation can trade off

An ensemble of incompatible models may predict better than any one model.

But incompatible accounts cannot jointly supply one coherent explanation of why the outcome occurs.

“Better model” therefore depends on the scientific aim.

McCain introduces this trade-off to qualify the simple claim that explanation-generated understanding improves prediction. It often does, but predictive success and explanatory success are not identical achievements and do not always increase together.

Begin with the difference in questions. Prediction asks what outcome should be expected, given some information. Explanation asks how or why the outcome depends on other features of the world. A model can discover a stable predictive pattern without representing the dependence relations that produce the outcome. Conversely, a model may correctly identify an important dependence or mechanism while remaining unable to forecast outcomes precisely because the system is complex, measurements are limited, or other influences vary unpredictably.

McCain’s example is robustness analysis in climate science. Researchers examine several models that make different—and sometimes mutually incompatible—assumptions about the system being modeled. Instead of treating one model as the uniquely correct representation, they compare or combine the models’ outputs. Agreement across models, or an ensemble constructed from them, can produce predictions more accurate or more robust than the prediction of any single model.

Why might combining models improve prediction? Different models can capture different regularities, compensate for one another’s errors, or reveal conclusions that do not depend on one specific set of modeling assumptions. Predictive aggregation can therefore work even when the models represent the underlying system differently.

The explanatory problem is incompatibility. Suppose Model A says an outcome depends on process A, while Model B relies on assumptions inconsistent with process A and attributes the outcome to process B. Their predictions can be averaged, compared, or treated as a range. Their explanations cannot simply be averaged into one coherent answer to “Why did this happen?” If the models disagree about the relevant entities, assumptions, mechanisms, or dependence relations, they cannot all literally describe the same causal structure. The ensemble may tell us what to expect while remaining silent or equivocal about which account makes the outcome intelligible.

This does not mean that:

  • accurate prediction never contributes to explanation;
  • explanatory models are generally poor predictors;
  • model ensembles provide no scientific knowledge; or
  • science must always choose explanation instead of prediction.

It means that predictive accuracy alone is insufficient evidence that a model explains. The appropriate model or modeling strategy depends partly on the purpose. A public agency deciding how much coastal infrastructure to build may prioritize a reliable predictive range. A scientist investigating why warming patterns occur may need a model whose assumptions and dependence relations can be interpreted as a coherent account of the system. Both projects are legitimate, but success in one should not be redescribed automatically as success in the other.

Social-science analogy: Imagine five machine-learning models that use hundreds of demographic, behavioral, and textual features to predict who will share political misinformation. An ensemble may predict sharing more accurately than any single model. Yet the models may rely on different features and incompatible representations of the relevant process. The ensemble can answer “Who is likely to share?” without answering whether sharing occurs because of identity protection, source cues, emotional arousal, network reinforcement, lack of knowledge, or some other dependence. A high-performing predictor is not thereby a theory2 explanation of the behavior.

Return to the seminar’s running example. A model could predict political tolerance from age, education, media use, ideology, network composition, and hundreds of linguistic features. It might predict accurately without showing that exchanging reasons produces tolerance through civic recognition. Conversely, evidence for that mechanism could yield genuine but incomplete understanding without allowing precise prediction of every participant’s response.

The deeper issue: Models are tools designed for purposes. Predictive models can tolerate complexity, opacity, and even incompatibility if those features improve forecasts. Explanatory models require an intelligible and sufficiently coherent representation of the dependence being claimed. Calling one model “better” is therefore incomplete until we specify: better for prediction, intervention, explanation, or some combination?

Discussion prompts:

  1. If an opaque ensemble predicts substantially better, when should social scientists prefer it?
  2. What additional evidence would be needed to turn a reliable predictor into an explanation?
  3. Can two incompatible models nevertheless illuminate different parts or levels of the same phenomenon?
  4. For the student projects in this course, which stages primarily require description, prediction, causal identification, or explanation?

Teaching shorthand: Prediction rewards getting the outcome right; explanation requires getting the dependence right. Sometimes one model—or one set of models—does one job better than the other.

Source: McCain (2022), chapter 1, pp. 3–4.
